What is an Italian moka pot and how does it work?

D
David Claes
Home barista & product tester
8 min read Updated: May 2025

Discover what an Italian moka pot is and exactly how it works. Learn everything about its mechanism, comparisons with espresso and filter coffee, and practical tips for making great coffee.

In this article, you will learn:

  • Exactly what an Italian moka pot is
  • How it works step by step (with a simple diagram)
  • How its taste compares to filter coffee and espresso
  • Practical tips to help you make excellent coffee

1. What is an Italian moka pot?

An Italian moka pot is a small metal coffee maker that sits on the hob (or induction hob, depending on the model). It typically consists of three main parts:

Bottom chamber – water reservoir

This is where you pour the water. Inside there is usually a safety valve: you fill the water just below it.

Middle section – funnel filter

This is a funnel-shaped metal filter in which you place the ground coffee. The coffee sits in a funnel with small holes at the base.

Top chamber – carafe

This is the carafe where the brewed coffee ends up. In the centre is a small tube through which the coffee rises.

Important: An Italian moka pot is often called a "percolator", but technically it differs from the old-style percolator where water circulates repeatedly through the same coffee. In the Italian moka pot, the water only passes once through the coffee upwards and stays there.

2. How does a moka pot work exactly? (Step by step)

The magic of the moka pot is actually simple physics: water + heat + pressure = coffee extraction.

Cross-section of a moka pot:

  • Bottom: water (heated by the hob)
  • Middle: layer of ground coffee in the filter
  • Top: empty chamber where the coffee flows to
  • Centre: a small tube from bottom to top (through which coffee is pushed)

As the water heats up, steam pressure builds in the lower chamber. This pressure forces the hot water up through the coffee grounds and into the upper chamber — creating a rich, concentrated coffee.

3. What type of coffee does a moka pot produce?

Coffee from an Italian moka pot sits in intensity between filter coffee and espresso:

Stronger and more concentrated than filter coffee

The coffee is fuller, with more body and often a more pronounced bitter note.

Less concentrated than a true espresso machine

The pressure in a moka pot is around 1–3 bar, while an espresso machine uses around 9 bar. You therefore get less crema and a slightly less syrupy texture.

Flavour profile:

The taste can be described as:

  • Full and intense, slightly syrupy
  • Often notes of cocoa, hazelnut and roasted aromas (especially with darker roasted beans)
  • With a typically Italian "punch" — ideal as a base for a cappuccino, latte or similar drinks

4. Moka pot vs filter coffee vs espresso machine

One of the most frequent questions is:

"Does moka coffee taste more like filter coffee or espresso?"

The most honest answer: it sits exactly in between.

5. Why do so many Italians choose a moka pot?

In Italy, the moka pot is more than an appliance: it's a ritual.

Affordable

A good moka pot is relatively inexpensive compared to an espresso machine.

Simple & robust

No electronics, no complicated menus. Just metal, a rubber gasket and a filter.

Compact

Takes up little space and travels easily on holiday or to a second home.

Iconic design

The classic octagonal shape (like the Bialetti) is a true design classic.

Ritual and atmosphere

The sound of the gurgling, the aroma filling the kitchen: everything contributes to the "Italian coffee at home" experience.

6. Practical tips for better moka coffee

To get the most out of your moka pot, follow these practical tips:

6.1 Choose the right coffee

  • Roast: Medium to dark roast works best
  • Grind: Moka pot / espresso grind, or grind yourself slightly finer than filter
  • Freshness: Use fresh beans and grind just before brewing

6.2 Water & temperature

  • Use fresh tap or filtered water (less limescale = better for taste and appliance)
  • Want less bitterness? Fill the reservoir with hot water from the kettle, so the moka pot spends less time on the heat

6.3 Heat & timing

  • Keep the flame low to medium. Too high = risk of burnt tastes
  • Remove the moka pot from the heat immediately as soon as you hear the hissing and the coffee is almost fully risen

6.4 Cleaning & maintenance

After use:

  • Let the moka pot cool slightly
  • Unscrew it, discard the coffee grounds and rinse all parts with hot water
  • Preferably no harsh detergent in the top chamber — many Italians believe a slight coffee patina improves the taste
  • Regularly check the rubber gasket and filter. Replace them if worn or no longer sealing properly

7. Summary: what to remember

  • An Italian moka pot consists of three parts: water reservoir, coffee filter and upper carafe
  • It works on steam pressure: hot water is pushed through the ground coffee upwards and concentrates in the upper chamber
  • The coffee is stronger than filter coffee but less concentrated than true espresso; ideal for those wanting a full-bodied, aromatic coffee at home without an expensive machine
  • Compared to filter coffee and espresso, moka coffee sits exactly in the middle in terms of pressure, taste and body, with a typically Italian character
  • With the right grind, moderate heat and good timing, a moka pot reliably produces a delicious Italian-style slow coffee
